# Focus Week: Take Control of Your Time

## Source
- Author: Cal Newport
- URL: https://calnewport.com/focus-week-take-control-of-your-time/
- Published: 2020-08-29 (modified 2023-01-26)
- Normalized file: `sources/newport-focus-week-time-blocking.md`
- Relevant workflow stages: **COMMIT** (primary: allocating a time block), **CLOSE** (re-planning after disruption; evidence of how long things take). Weak support for ESTIMATE.

## Core principles

### DIRECT
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-01** — Take control of your time: when thinking about the work day, **give every minute a job**. *(opening; "Here's the basic idea")*
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-02** — Time blocking means partitioning the day into blocks and assigning specific work to each block. Example: a strategy memo 9:00–10:00, a meeting 10:00–10:30, email for 30 minutes, then 90 minutes (11:00–12:30) on a project report that's due. *("Here's the basic idea")*
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-03** — If an unexpected crisis or an overrunning task knocks you off the schedule, build an updated time-block schedule for the rest of the day at the next chance you get. The key is keeping *intention* about your time, not *perfection* in planning. *(parenthetical in "Here's the basic idea")*
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-04** — The list/reactive method has two problems. (1) Letting other people's needs drive your activities skews urgent vs important: you feel busy but don't move what matters. (2) With no plan beyond "trying to get things done," ad hoc internet "breaks" turn into time-devouring rabbit holes. *(two-problems paragraphs)*
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-05** — Time blocking gives fine-grained control over the balance of urgent and important. Knowing what you're supposed to be doing makes unplanned breaks less likely. *(paragraph after the two problems)*
- **CLOSE-TBLOCK-01** — The scheduling commitment gives hard evidence of how much time you really have and how long things really take. That reality check leads to "more vigorous essentialism" and more conservatism about how early you start projects. *("This scheduling commitment also provides…")*
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-06** — Time blocking is cognitively demanding. Don't extend it to time outside work; that rigidity leads to burnout. *("A word of warning")*
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-07** — Time blocking isn't enough by itself. You also need regular time to reflect on *what* to focus on in your work. *(closing paragraphs)*

### INFERRED
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-08** — A block is assigned *specific* work, so a vague "work on stuff" block doesn't satisfy the method. *(from "assign specific work to these blocks")*
- **CLOSE-TBLOCK-02** — When reality diverges from the plan, the correct response is to re-plan the rest of the day, not to abandon planning or treat the plan as failed. *(from the "knocked off your schedule" parenthetical)*
- **ESTIMATE-TBLOCK-01** — The "hard evidence on… how long things really take" produced by time blocking can feed later estimates. Newport doesn't describe any mechanism for recording or using it. Connecting it to EBS's estimate-vs-actual calibration is an **INFERRED bridge**, not something the source states. *(from "This scheduling commitment…")*

## Procedure / workflow
The source does not give a numbered procedure. It describes the practice (INFERRED sequence, not DIRECT):
1. Partition the work day into blocks and assign specific work to each. Every minute gets a job.
2. Work the blocks.
3. If knocked off by a crisis or an overrun, rebuild the schedule for the rest of the day at the next chance.
4. (Implicitly) notice how much time you really had and how long things took.

## Decision rules and gates
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-09** — Rule: if the day is disrupted, re-plan the remainder. Don't drop the plan and don't chase a perfect plan. *(DIRECT, "Here's the basic idea")*
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-10** — Boundary: apply block discipline to work time only, not personal time. *(DIRECT, "A word of warning")*
- The source defines **no stop condition** for the task inside a block. It doesn't say what to do when the block ends and the task isn't done, except to rebuild the schedule. See limitations.

## Questions the future agent could ask
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-Q01** `[INFERRED]` — What specific work is this block for? *("assign specific work to these blocks")*
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-Q02** `[INFERRED]` — Is this block serving what's important, or reacting to someone else's urgent request? *(two problems of list/reactive)*
- **CLOSE-TBLOCK-Q01** `[INFERRED]` — You got knocked off the plan. What does the rest of today look like now? *("knocked off your schedule")*
- **CLOSE-TBLOCK-Q02** `[INFERRED]` — How long did that really take compared with the block you gave it? *("hard evidence… how long things really take")*
- **COMMIT-TBLOCK-Q03** `[DERIVED]` — What will you do if the block ends before the slice is done: extend, re-plan, or stop and capture the state?

## Positive examples from the source

### COMMIT-TBLOCK-P01 — A blocked morning
**Classification:** DIRECT
**Source location:** "Here's the basic idea"
**What happens:** Strategy memo 9:00–10:00. Meeting 10:00–10:30. Thirty minutes for email. Then 11:00–12:30 on a project report that's due soon.
**Why it is positive:** Each period has an assigned, specific job, including reactive work (email), which is contained in its own block.
**Principle illustrated:** COMMIT-TBLOCK-01, -02.

### CLOSE-TBLOCK-P01 — Re-plan after disruption
**Classification:** DIRECT
**Source location:** "Here's the basic idea" (parenthetical)
**What happens:** A crisis or an overrunning task knocks you off the schedule. You build an updated schedule for the rest of the day at the next chance.
**Why it is positive:** It keeps intention without demanding perfection.
**Principle illustrated:** COMMIT-TBLOCK-03.

## Negative examples / anti-patterns from the source

### COMMIT-TBLOCK-N01 — List/reactive day
**Classification:** DIRECT
**Source location:** "Here's the basic idea"; two-problems paragraphs
**What happens:** Time between meetings is filled by reacting to email and occasionally, "when the mood hits you," picking items from an unwieldy task list.
**Why it is negative:** Other people's needs drive the day and urgent crowds out important. You feel busy and exhausted without moving what matters. Unplanned breaks become rabbit holes.
**Principle violated:** COMMIT-TBLOCK-01, -04.

### COMMIT-TBLOCK-N02 — Blocking your whole life
**Classification:** DIRECT
**Source location:** "A word of warning"
**What happens:** Block discipline is extended to time outside work.
**Why it is negative:** That excessive rigidity eventually leads to burnout.
**Principle violated:** COMMIT-TBLOCK-06.

## Contrastive pairs

### COMMIT-TBLOCK-C01 — How the day is driven
**Good / preferred:** Time blocking: partition the day, assign specific work to blocks, every minute has a job.
**Bad / discouraged:** List/reactive: react to email between meetings and pluck tasks from a list when the mood hits.
**Classification:** DIRECT
**Source location:** "Here's the basic idea"; following paragraphs
**Distinguishing feature:** Whether time is allocated intentionally in advance or consumed reactively.

### CLOSE-TBLOCK-C02 — Responding to disruption
**Good / preferred:** Rebuild the schedule for the rest of the day. Intention over perfection.
**Bad / discouraged:** *(DERIVED; the source doesn't name the opposite)* Abandon the plan for the day once it breaks, or insist on a perfect plan.
**Classification:** Good side DIRECT; bad side DERIVED
**Source location:** "Here's the basic idea" (parenthetical)
**Distinguishing feature:** Whether a broken plan is repaired or discarded.

## Stop / go criteria

### GO
- The block has specific work assigned. *(DIRECT)*
- The work is during working time. *(DIRECT, warning)*

### STOP / RETURN
- Knocked off the schedule: stop and re-plan the rest of the day at the next chance. *(DIRECT)*
- Unsure *what* to focus on: time blocking won't answer that. Reflection is needed separately. *(DIRECT, "not enough by itself")*
- The source gives **no** stop condition for the task inside a block.

## Common failure modes
- Reactive days driven by other people's needs (COMMIT-TBLOCK-N01).
- Unplanned breaks becoming rabbit holes (COMMIT-TBLOCK-04).
- Extending blocking to personal time and burning out (COMMIT-TBLOCK-N02).
- Treating time blocking as enough without deciding *what* matters (COMMIT-TBLOCK-07).

## Terms worth preserving
- `time blocking` — partitioning the work day into blocks with specific work assigned to each.
- `give every minute a job` — the core rule.
- `list/reactive method` — the contrasting default: react to messages and pull from a task list.
- `intention` vs `perfection` — what the plan should preserve (intention) and what it need not achieve (perfection).
- `rabbit holes` — *here* means time-devouring internet "breaks." This is **not** Shape Up's rabbit holes (unresolved risks in shaped work). Don't conflate them.

## Candidate Skill rules
- `[DERIVED from "Here's the basic idea"]` Before implementing, assign the slice to a specific time block. Don't let it be "whenever I get to it."
- `[DERIVED from "Here's the basic idea"]` If an interruption or overrun breaks the block, re-plan the rest of the day. Don't silently continue or abandon the plan.
- `[DERIVED from two-problems paragraphs]` Contain reactive work (messages, review requests) in its own block instead of letting it fill the gaps.
- `[DERIVED from "This scheduling commitment…"]` After a block, note how long the work actually took compared with the block. (Using this for calibration is an EBS bridge, not Newport's.)
- `[DERIVED from "not enough by itself"]` Do not use a time block to decide *what* to work on. That decision belongs to CLARIFY/SLICE.
- `[DERIVED from "A word of warning"]` Don't push block discipline outside working hours.

## Source limitations
- **A time block is neither an estimate nor a delivery commitment.** It allocates attention to work. It says nothing about whether the work will be done within the block, and it isn't a promise to anyone else. Don't equate it with an EBS estimate or a Shape Up appetite.
- **No stop condition** for the work inside a block. What to do when the block ends with the task unfinished isn't addressed beyond "build an updated schedule."
- **No mechanism** for recording or using the "hard evidence" about durations. Any calibration loop has to come from EBS.
- General knowledge-work advice, not software-specific. It doesn't address slicing, testing, or technical uncertainty.
- The piece belongs to a series (Focus Week lessons 1–2 not fetched) and promotes a book, planner, and course. Only the method paragraphs are used as grounding.

## Derived developer examples

### COMMIT-TBLOCK-D01 — Interruption mid-block
**Classification:** DERIVED
**Grounded in:** Newport, "Here's the basic idea" (COMMIT-TBLOCK-03, CLOSE-TBLOCK-C02); CLOSE-EBS-03 for the clock-running bridge
**Preferred:** A 10:00–11:30 block is for "add server-side email validation to signup form." At 10:40 a production-bug page comes in and takes until 11:50. Afterwards the developer rewrites the afternoon: the validation slice moves to 13:30–14:30 and the review block is pushed. The EBS clock stays running on the validation task *if* the interruption is being treated per EBS.
**Discouraged:** After the page, the developer drifts between Slack, the bug follow-up, and half-opening the validation branch for the rest of the day with no new plan.
**Distinguishing feature:** Whether the broken plan is rebuilt for the rest of the day.

### COMMIT-TBLOCK-D02 — Specific vs vague block
**Classification:** DERIVED
**Grounded in:** Newport, "assign specific work to these blocks" (COMMIT-TBLOCK-02, -08)
**Preferred:** 14:00–15:30: "Make the `ProfileCard` Vue component show the verified badge; test for the verified and unverified cases."
**Discouraged:** 14:00–17:00: "Frontend stuff."
**Distinguishing feature:** Whether the block names specific work.
